Welcome / Training Overview

Who is this session for?

This workshop is designed for participants from diverse academic or professional backgrounds, including those with limited prior experience in mathematics, statistics, data analysis, or AI.

The session introduces a guided workflow and practical examples. It does not assume advanced technical knowledge and does not aim to create expert-level competency within two hours.

Session Principle

AI should support thinking, not replace it.

Use AI to explore, question, clarify and evaluate ideas. Verify important academic information, inspect evidence, and keep final academic judgement with the user.

Introduction / Basic Statistics Primer

Before We Start: Basic Ideas You Should Understand

This section gives a simple introduction to some very basic statistics ideas needed for the sharing session. The aim is not to go very deep, but to help participants recognise common data types, measurement scales, and basic sampling terms before moving into analysis.

Why this matters: before choosing an analysis or building a model, we must first understand what type of data we have, how the variables are measured, and how the sample was obtained.
Data formText, numbers, pictures/images, sound/audio and mixed forms.
Data natureQualitative (categorical) and quantitative (numerical).
Measurement scaleNominal, ordinal, binary and Likert-scale data.
Quantitative typeDiscrete and continuous numerical data.
SamplingSimple idea of population, sample, sampling technique and sample size.
Mixed dataDatasets often contain both categorical and numerical variables.

Data can appear in many forms

Before analysis, recognise the form in which information is collected or stored. Different forms may need different tools or different handling.

📝
Text

Comments, names, written responses, feedback, short answers.

🔢
Numbers

Age, income, marks, rainfall, temperature, number of items.

🖼️
Picture / Image

X-rays, satellite images, photos, scanned forms or visual records.

🔊
Sound / Audio

Speech recording, interview audio, machine sound or heartbeat sound.

🧩
Mixed Data

A dataset can contain text, numbers and images together in one project.

Qualitative / Categorical

These data describe qualities, groups or labels rather than numerical amount.

  • Typical question: Which group?
  • Examples: gender, faculty, religion, blood group, satisfaction level.
  • Common subtypes: nominal, ordinal, binary, Likert-scale responses.
↔

Quantitative / Numerical

These data measure amount, size, count or magnitude using numbers.

  • Typical question: How much? How many?
  • Examples: age, score, weight, rainfall, number of siblings.
  • Common subtypes: discrete and continuous.
Simple memory tip: if the value acts like a label/category, it is usually qualitative. If the value tells us how much or how many, it is usually quantitative.

Categorical Variables

Nominal

Categories with no natural order. Example: programme, race, blood group.

Ordinal

Categories with an order. Example: low, medium, high.

Binary

Only two categories. Example: yes/no, pass/fail.

Likert

Scale such as strongly disagree to strongly agree; commonly treated as ordinal in basic statistics.

↔

Numerical Variables

Discrete

Countable numbers. Example: number of children, number of absences.

Continuous

Measured values. Example: height, time, rainfall, blood pressure.

Whole number focus

Discrete values often appear as 0, 1, 2, 3 and so on.

Can take decimals

Continuous values may take many possible values within a range.

Level of Measurement

The level of measurement tells us how much information a variable carries. This matters because it influences which summaries and analyses are appropriate.

NominalLabels only
OrdinalLabels + order
IntervalOrder + equal intervals
RatioEqual intervals + true zero
Level 1

Nominal

  • Key idea: labels or names only.
  • Order? No.
  • Examples: gender, faculty, blood group, marital status.
  • Useful summary: frequency or percentage.
Level 2

Ordinal

  • Key idea: categories have an order.
  • Equal gap? Not guaranteed.
  • Examples: low–medium–high, class position, Likert responses.
  • Useful summary: median, rank, frequency.
Level 3

Interval

  • Key idea: differences are meaningful and intervals are equal.
  • True zero? No true zero point.
  • Examples: temperature in °C or °F, calendar year.
  • Important note: 20°C is not “twice as hot” as 10°C.
Level 4

Ratio

  • Key idea: equal intervals and a meaningful zero.
  • Examples: height, weight, age, income, rainfall amount.
  • Why special? Ratios make sense: 20 kg is twice 10 kg.
  • Common use: many numerical analyses use ratio-level data.
Quick guide: nominal = name, ordinal = order, interval = order + equal interval, ratio = interval + true zero.

the total number in the full group of interest.

the number actually selected for analysis.

Reminder

Sampling technique

Simple Random Sampling

Every member has an equal chance of being selected.

Good for fairness when a full sampling frame is available.
Sampling technique

Systematic Sampling

Select every k-th observation after a starting point.

Example: every 5th name on a list.
Sampling technique

Stratified Sampling

Divide the population into subgroups, then sample from each subgroup.

Useful when subgroups must be represented.
Sampling technique

Cluster Sampling

Select whole groups or clusters instead of individuals.

Example: choose several classes, then survey all students in those classes.
Sampling technique

Convenience Sampling

Choose the people who are easiest to reach.

Fast and easy, but often less representative.
Sampling technique

Purposive Sampling

Select cases that fit a specific purpose or criterion.

Often used when the study needs specific types of participants.

Complete Research Workflow

ETL → DEPLOYMENT

Follow the complete workflow from raw data extraction through modelling, validation and deployment. Click each tab to explore the role of that stage.

01 — ETL

ETL means Extract, Transform and Load. This is the practical stage where you bring raw data into a usable working file.

01Extract
02Inspect format
03Transform basic format
04Load into software
05Save raw + working copy

What do I actually do?

  1. Extract the data from a source such as data.gov.my, OpenDOSM, World Bank, UCI, an Excel file or CSV file.
  2. Check the file format and whether all required sheets, columns and records are present.
  3. Transform obvious formatting issues, such as dates stored as text or numbers stored with symbols.
  4. Load the cleaned working copy into Excel, SPSS, R, Python or Power BI.
  5. Keep the original raw file unchanged so you can always return to it.
Example: Download a CSV from OpenDOSM, save the original as raw_data.csv, then create working_data.xlsx for analysis.

Quick check before moving on

02 — Data Inspection & Understanding

Before cleaning or analysing, understand what each column means and how the dataset was collected.

01Read metadata
02Identify variables
03Check data types
04Check units
05Confirm context

What do I actually do?

  1. Read the dataset description, metadata, codebook or source notes.
  2. Identify each variable name and its meaning.
  3. Identify the data type: text/categorical, numerical, date/time, binary, ordinal, etc.
  4. Check units such as RM, kg, mm, percentage or years.
  5. Check the number of rows and columns.
  6. Identify the population, observation unit and time period.
Example: If one row represents one student, make sure you do not mistakenly interpret one row as one class or one school.

Quick check before moving on

03 — Data Cleaning & Pre-processing

Data cleaning means finding and fixing problems that could distort the analysis. Do not change values blindly—every cleaning decision should have a reason.

01Find problems
02Verify the problem
03Decide what to do
04Apply the change
05Document it

What do I actually do?

  1. Check missing values: blank cells, NA, N/A, -, 999 or other codes that may mean 'missing'.
  2. Check duplicates: repeated rows or repeated IDs that should occur only once.
  3. Check data types: ensure numeric variables are stored as numbers, dates as dates and categories consistently.
  4. Standardise category labels: e.g. 'Male', 'male', 'M' should be reviewed and made consistent if they mean the same thing.
  5. Check impossible or invalid values: e.g. age = -5, percentage = 140, height = 900 cm.
  6. Inspect outliers: verify whether an extreme value is a real observation or a data-entry error.
  7. Remove unnecessary columns only if they are not needed for the research question.
  8. Document every change made to the dataset.
Example: In Excel, use Filter, Sort, Remove Duplicates, Find/Replace, COUNTBLANK, Conditional Formatting and basic summary statistics to identify common problems.
Before
Gender column contains: Male, male, M, Female, F.
After
After verification: Male and Female are coded consistently.

Quick check before moving on

04 — Feature Engineering

Feature engineering means creating or transforming variables so they are more useful for the research question or model.

01Define need
02Create variable
03Check formula
04Verify values
05Document

What do I actually do?

  1. Create derived variables only when they have a clear meaning.
  2. Combine existing variables when theoretically justified.
  3. Recode categories where necessary, while keeping a record of the original coding.
  4. Create ratios, percentages or rates if these are more meaningful than raw counts.
  5. Transform variables only if the analysis requires it.
  6. Do not create a new variable simply because software allows it.
Example: From monthly income and household size, you might create income per household member if that quantity is relevant to the research question.

Quick check before moving on

05 — Descriptive Analysis

Descriptive analysis summarises what the data look like before you perform inferential analysis.

01Summarise
02Visualise
03Compare
04Spot anomalies
05Describe

What do I actually do?

  1. For numerical data, examine mean, median, minimum, maximum and standard deviation where appropriate.
  2. For categorical data, examine frequencies and percentages.
  3. Use charts such as histogram, bar chart, box plot or scatter plot according to variable type.
  4. Compare summary statistics with the raw data to detect suspicious values.
  5. Describe the sample before making conclusions.
Example: For a score variable, report n, mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum, then inspect a histogram or box plot.

Quick check before moving on

06 — Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

EDA is used to explore patterns, relationships and unusual observations before formal modelling.

01Plot distributions
02Compare groups
03Explore relationships
04Identify anomalies
05Generate questions

What do I actually do?

  1. Plot distributions of key variables.
  2. Use scatter plots for relationships between two numerical variables.
  3. Use grouped summaries to compare categories.
  4. Look for non-linear patterns, clusters and unusual observations.
  5. Check correlations carefully and remember that correlation does not establish causation.
  6. Use EDA to generate questions, not to force a conclusion.
Example: A scatter plot of study hours and test score may suggest a positive relationship, but you still need formal analysis and context before drawing conclusions.

Quick check before moving on

07 — Inference Analysis & Model Selection

This stage moves from description to statistical inference or modelling. The method should follow the research question and variable structure.

01Question
02Choose variables
03Choose method
04Check assumptions
05Fit & interpret

What do I actually do?

  1. State the research question clearly.
  2. Identify the outcome variable and predictor/explanatory variables.
  3. Choose an analysis that matches the variable types and study design.
  4. Check whether assumptions are reasonable.
  5. Fit the model or conduct the statistical test.
  6. Compare candidate models only when there is a valid reason to do so.
  7. Interpret effect size, uncertainty and statistical evidence together.
Example: If Y is a continuous score and X is study hours, simple linear regression may be considered if the relationship and assumptions are reasonable.

Quick check before moving on

08 — Model Performance & Validation

A model should not be accepted only because it produces a p-value or a high R². Check whether the model behaves reasonably.

01Check diagnostics
02Check assumptions
03Measure performance
04Validate
05Report limitations

What do I actually do?

  1. Inspect residuals and diagnostic plots.
  2. Check whether key model assumptions are reasonably satisfied.
  3. Look for influential observations.
  4. Evaluate model fit using appropriate measures such as R², adjusted R², RMSE or MAE where relevant.
  5. For predictive work, assess performance on data not used to fit the model when possible.
  6. Compare performance with a simple baseline where appropriate.
  7. Report weaknesses or uncertainty rather than hiding them.
Example: A regression model with high R² can still be misleading if residuals show strong patterns or a few extreme observations dominate the fit.

Quick check before moving on

09 — Deployment

Deployment means turning the analysis into something usable. In academic work, this may be a report, dashboard, reproducible file, decision-support tool or presentation.

01Choose output
02Communicate findings
03Add limitations
04Check reproducibility
05Share/update

What do I actually do?

  1. Decide who will use the output and for what purpose.
  2. Create a clear report, dashboard, visualisation or application.
  3. Include the data source, method, key findings and limitations.
  4. Make the workflow reproducible where possible.
  5. Protect confidential or sensitive data.
  6. Update the output if new data become available.
Example: A validated analysis may be presented as an Excel dashboard, Power BI dashboard, research report or interactive web application.

B. Discover Ideas with AI

🔎 Unfamiliar topics
🏷️ Important keywords
🔗 Related concepts
⚖️ Different perspectives

How to Use DISCOVER

1. Start Broad

Ask for an overview before requesting conclusions.

2. Identify Concepts

Find terminology, theories, variables, methods and debates.

3. Explore Perspectives

Ask how different disciplines or researchers may view the topic.

4. Generate Directions

Brainstorm possibilities without treating them as final decisions.

5. Identify Gaps

Ask what may be missing or what information is still needed.

Example

Topic: Student engagement in online learning

Basic

“What are the main factors commonly discussed in relation to student engagement in online learning?”

Follow-up

“Group these factors into student-related, teacher-related, technology-related, and course-design factors.”

Then

“Which of these factors could reasonably be investigated using survey data?”

Purpose: not to decide which factor is correct, but to discover the possibilities.

Try Asking AI

Prompt 1 — Explore a Topic

I am new to [TOPIC]. Give me a structured overview of the main concepts, important terminology, and related ideas I should understand before studying it in detail.

Prompt 2 — Generate Keywords

Generate 15 useful academic search keywords and keyword combinations related to [TOPIC]. Group them into broad, specific, and methodological terms.

Prompt 3 — Explore Perspectives

Show me several different perspectives or approaches to understanding [TOPIC]. Explain briefly how each perspective differs.

Prompt 4 — Identify Gaps

Based on this topic, what important questions or areas might someone overlook? Do not assume these are proven research gaps; present them as possible areas for further investigation.

Prompt 5 — Map the Topic

Create a beginner-friendly concept map in text form for [TOPIC], showing the main concept, related concepts, possible variables, and possible applications.

💡 KEY TIP

Do not stop at the first AI response. Ask follow-up questions, request alternative perspectives, and compare different explanations. AI can help expand the search space, but you remain responsible for deciding what is relevant and worth investigating.

Stage 02

EVALUATE

Can I trust or use this information?

Evaluate means critically examining information before deciding whether it is reliable, relevant, and appropriate to use.

Important distinction

AI-GENERATED SUGGESTION ≠ VERIFIED ACADEMIC EVIDENCE

AI-generated information should not automatically be treated as verified fact.

Accuracy

Is the information factually correct?

Relevance

Does it actually answer the question?

Credibility

Is it supported by trustworthy expertise or reliable sources?

Evidence

What evidence supports the statement?

Currency

Is the information current enough?

Bias

Is one perspective presented as if it were the only valid view?

Consistency

Does it agree with other credible sources?

Limitations / Source Quality

What uncertainty, weakness or source problem is missing?

Example

AI claim:

“Online learning always increases student independence.”

→ What evidence supports this claim?

→ Is the word “always” justified?

→ What conditions might produce different outcomes?

Important claims should then be checked against suitable academic or authoritative sources.

Before accepting an AI response, ask:

Try Asking AI

Prompt 1 — Critical Review

Review the following response critically. Separate factual claims, interpretations, assumptions, and recommendations. Identify which claims require external verification.

Prompt 2 — Find Weaknesses

Identify possible weaknesses, unsupported assumptions, missing context, or overgeneralisation in the following explanation: [PASTE TEXT].

Prompt 3 — Compare Explanations

Compare these two explanations of [TOPIC]. Identify where they agree, where they differ, and what evidence would be needed to determine which interpretation is better supported.

Prompt 4 — Evidence Check

For each major claim in the following text, tell me what type of evidence or source would be appropriate for verification. Do not invent references.

Prompt 5 — Bias and Alternatives

Examine the following response for possible bias or one-sided framing. Suggest alternative interpretations or perspectives that should also be considered.

⚠️ KEY TIP

Fluent language is not evidence of accuracy. A response can sound professional and still be wrong. Treat important academic claims as something to verify, not simply something to believe.

Stage 03

UNDERSTAND

Do I really understand it?

Understand means moving beyond receiving an answer to understanding the meaning, reasoning, relationships, and implications behind it.

ANSWER→MEANING→APPLICATION
🧠 SIMPLIFY

Translate technical ideas into clearer language.

🔄 RE-EXPLAIN

Explain the same idea using another approach.

⚖️ COMPARE

Show similarities and differences.

📚 GIVE EXAMPLES

Connect abstract ideas to realistic situations.

🔗 CONNECT IDEAS

Relate new knowledge to prior knowledge.

⚠️ MISCONCEPTIONS

Identify common misunderstandings.

💡 ANALOGIES

Use intuitive comparisons while noting their limits.

🪜 BREAK DOWN

Present complex processes as manageable steps.

Example

Concept: Correlation does not imply causation

Basic: “Define correlation.”
Better: “Explain why correlation does not prove causation using a simple example.”
Deeper: “What alternative explanations could produce a correlation between two variables?”
Further: “Give me a situation where causal interpretation might be more defensible.”

From Answer → To Understanding

Answer-focused

“What is a confidence interval?”

Understanding-focused

“Explain what a confidence interval means in practical terms and identify one common misconception.”

Deeper understanding

“Give me two examples showing correct and incorrect interpretations of a 95% confidence interval.”

The first question obtains information. The later questions encourage understanding.

Try Asking AI

Prompt 1 — Beginner Explanation

Explain [CONCEPT] to a beginner using simple language, but keep the important academic meaning.

Prompt 2 — Explain With an Example

Explain [CONCEPT] using one simple real-world example. Then explain exactly how the example represents the concept.

Prompt 3 — Compare Concepts

Compare [CONCEPT A] and [CONCEPT B]. Explain their similarities, differences, and when each is used.

Prompt 4 — Find Misconceptions

What common misconceptions might a beginner have about [CONCEPT]? Explain why each misconception is incorrect.

Prompt 5 — Explain Another Way

I still do not understand this explanation: [PASTE TEXT]. Explain it in a different way using simpler language, an analogy, and a short example.

🧠 KEY TIP

If you can only repeat the AI's answer, you may not yet understand it. Ask: Why? How? Can you give me an example? What would be a counterexample? How is this different from something similar? What mistake might I make here?

Stage 04

QUESTION

What should I ask next?

Better questioning improves thinking, not just AI output. It helps clarify ideas, expose assumptions, identify evidence, recognise uncertainty, compare alternatives and move from description to analysis.

A useful questioning progression

RECALL

What is it?

EXPLAIN

Why does it happen?

APPLY

ANALYSE

How are the parts related?

EVALUATE

How strong is the evidence?

CREATE

What could I propose or investigate?

This is a useful progression, not a rigid rule. Choose the level that fits your learning purpose.

Clarification

“What exactly does this term mean?”

Probing

“Why does this conclusion follow?”

Comparison

“How is Method A different from Method B?”

Why

“Why is this assumption important?”

What If

“What might happen if this condition is violated?”

Assumption Checking

“What assumptions does this argument depend on?”

Evidence Seeking

“What evidence would support this claim?”

Reflective

“What have I understood well, and what remains unclear?”

Weak → Better → Deeper

Regression

Weak: “What is regression?”

Better: “Explain the purpose of regression and give a simple real-world example.”

Deeper: “What assumptions should be considered before interpreting a regression model, and what problems could arise if they are violated?”

AI Bias

Weak: “What is AI bias?”

Better: “Explain how bias may appear in AI-generated responses and give two examples.”

Deeper: “How could a user distinguish between bias introduced by the training data, the prompt, and the interpretation of the AI response?”

Source Evaluation

Weak: “Is this source good?”

Better: “Evaluate the credibility of this source using authority, evidence, currency, relevance, and possible bias.”

Deeper: “What additional sources would you consult before relying on this source for an academic conclusion?”

1

Regression in Plain English

Regression describes how an outcome variable changes in relation to one or more predictor variables. In simple linear regression, one predictor \(X\) is related to one numerical outcome \(Y\).

Example question

Can study hours help explain or predict students' test scores?

Y: Test Score
X: Study Hours
🧠 Remember

Association or prediction does not automatically establish causation.

2

When Should I Use Regression?

Simple Linear Regression

One numerical outcome \(Y\) and one predictor \(X\).

Multiple Linear Regression

One numerical outcome \(Y\) and two or more predictors.

⚠️ Regression may not be suitable when...

the outcome is not numerical, the relationship is clearly non-linear, data quality is poor, or the study design does not support the intended interpretation.

Good to know: other regression types

The source notes also cover logistic regression for categorical outcomes and multivariate regression for multiple outcomes. They are useful next steps, but the core training here focuses on basic linear regression.

3

Meet the Regression Equation

\[\hat{y}=b_0+b_1x\]
\(\hat y\)
predicted outcome
\(b_0\)
intercept
\(b_1\)
slope / regression coefficient
\(x\)
predictor value

Hypothetical training example

\(\hat y=45+3.2x\)

Each additional study hour is associated with an estimated 3.2-point increase in predicted test score.

Predicted score: 61.0

4

Look at Your Data First

✓ Identify Y and X.

✓ Inspect missing values and obvious errors.

✓ Review descriptive statistics.

✓ Create a scatter plot.

✓ Look for unusual observations / outliers.

✓ Ask whether an approximately linear pattern is plausible.

🛑 STOP & CHECK

Software can print an output even when the model is inappropriate.

5

Run the Regression

  1. 1. Select outcome Y.
  2. 2. Select predictor(s) X.
  3. 3. Fit the model.
  4. 4. Examine coefficient, \(R^2\), p-value and/or confidence interval.
  5. 5. Check diagnostics.
  6. 6. Interpret and report.

The same conceptual workflow applies in R, Python, SPSS, Jamovi, Excel and other statistical software.

6

The Regression Output: Focus Here First

1. Coefficient

Direction and estimated size of change in Y.

2. \(R^2\)

Variation in Y accounted for by the model.

3. p-value / CI

Evidence about whether a coefficient differs from zero.

4. Direction

Positive = upward; negative = downward.

5. Diagnostics

Look for residual patterns and unusual points.

⚠️ Safeguard

p < 0.05 does not prove practical importance, good model fit or causation. High \(R^2\) does not automatically mean a model is valid.

7

How to Interpret Results

Coefficient

“For every one-unit increase in X, predicted Y increases/decreases by approximately ___ units.”

\(R^2\)

“Approximately ___% of observed variation in Y is accounted for by the predictors in this model.”

p-value

A small p-value is evidence against a zero coefficient under the model assumptions; it is not a measure of practical importance.

Prediction

Prefer interpolation within the observed range; extrapolation outside it is risky.

8

🚦 Regression Health Check

✓ Linearity

A straight-line pattern should be a reasonable approximation.

✓ Independence

Problematic dependence should not be present where independence is assumed.

✓ Constant residual variance

Residual spread should be reasonably similar across fitted values.

✓ Residual behaviour

Residual and Q–Q plots can reveal warning signs.

✓ Influential observations

Extreme points can strongly affect the fitted model.

✓ Multicollinearity

In multiple regression, highly correlated predictors can make coefficient interpretation less reliable.

9

Multiple Regression Without the Fear

\[\hat y=b_0+b_1x_1+b_2x_2+\cdots+b_kx_k\]

One numerical outcome, several predictors.

House-price example
  • Y = House price
  • \(X_1\) = House size
  • \(X_2\) = Number of bedrooms
  • \(X_3\) = Distance from city centre

“Holding the other predictors constant” means interpreting one predictor while comparing cases that are similar on the other predictors in the model.

Advanced / Next Step: model selection

The source notes introduce adjusted \(R^2\), Mallows' \(C_p\), AIC, all-possible regression, best-subset regression and stepwise regression. Keep these as advanced tools rather than the beginner starting point.

10

From Output to a Research Statement

❌ Poor

“X causes Y because p < 0.05.”

✅ Better

“X was statistically associated with Y in the fitted model. This result should not automatically be interpreted as causal.”

Reusable reporting template

“A linear regression was used to examine whether [X] was associated with / helped predict [Y]. The estimated coefficient for [X] was [b = ___], indicating that [...]. The model accounted for approximately [\(R^2\times100\)]% of the observed variation in [Y]. [Include uncertainty/significance information where appropriate]. These results indicate an association within the analysed data and should not automatically be interpreted as causal.”

Cleaning checks in Excel

  1. Check blank cells with filters or COUNTBLANK.

What would count as a cleaning problem?

  • Missing: empty G3 cell.
  • Impossible: G3 = 28 although the documented scale is 0–20.
  • Inconsistent: “yes”, “Yes” and “Y” used for the same category.
  • Duplicate: the same record entered twice unintentionally.

Complete Research Report Example

Title: Modelling Final Student Performance Using Prior Grades, Study Time, Previous Failures and Absences: A Multiple Linear Regression Example Using the UCI Student Performance Dataset

Purpose: This worked report demonstrates how regression output can be translated into an academically cautious research narrative. Numerical results below are taken from the published regression example used earlier in this workbook.

1. Background and Objective

Student performance can be explored using academic and behavioural variables. The UCI Student Performance dataset contains information from two Portuguese secondary schools, including demographic, social, school-related and grade variables. For this worked example, the final grade G3 is treated as the numerical outcome. The objective is to examine the association between G3 and five predictors: first-period grade (G1), second-period grade (G2), weekly study-time category, previous class failures and school absences.

Research question: To what extent are G1, G2, study time, previous failures and absences associated with students' final grade (G3) in the fitted multiple linear regression model?
2. Data and Variables

The Portuguese-language file in the UCI Student Performance dataset contains 649 observations and 33 variables. UCI documents G1, G2 and G3 on a 0–20 scale, studytime as an ordered category from 1 to 4, failures as the number of past class failures, and absences as the number of school absences.

3. Statistical Method

A multiple linear regression model was fitted with G3 as the dependent variable and G1, G2, studytime, failures and absences as predictors. The fitted equation reported in the published example is:

G3 = −0.15519 + 0.13946(G1) + 0.88571(G2) + 0.09670(studytime) − 0.21829(failures) + 0.02337(absences) + error

Each coefficient is interpreted as the estimated change in G3 for a one-unit increase in that predictor, holding the other predictors in the model constant. These are model-based associations and should not automatically be interpreted as causal effects.

Multiple R² = 0.8506Approximately 85.06% of the observed variation in G3 is accounted for by the five predictors in this fitted sample model. This is not the same as saying that the predictors cause 85.06% of student performance.
Adjusted R² = 0.8494The adjusted value remains close to R², indicating that the reported explanatory fit is not materially reduced after accounting for the number of predictors included.
Residual Standard Error = 1.254The fitted residuals have a typical scale of roughly 1.25 grade points. This should be interpreted together with residual diagnostics rather than as a stand-alone guarantee of predictive accuracy.
F(5,643) = 732, p < 2.2×10⁻¹⁶The model provides strong evidence that the predictor set collectively improves on an intercept-only model. The F test does not establish causality or guarantee that every individual predictor is useful.

7. Main Findings
  1. G2 is the dominant fitted predictor on its original scale. A one-point increase in G2 is associated with approximately 0.886 additional G3 points, conditional on the other predictors.
  2. G1 also contributes positively. Its fitted association is smaller than G2 once G2 and the other variables are included.
  3. Previous failures have a negative fitted association. More previous failures correspond to lower predicted final grades, all else equal in the model.
  4. Studytime is not statistically distinguishable from zero at the 0.05 level in this fitted model. This does not prove that study time is unimportant; it means this model, with these covariates and coding, does not provide strong evidence for a non-zero coefficient.
  5. The positive absence coefficient is small and counter-intuitive. It should be treated as a model-specific adjusted association rather than evidence that absence improves performance.
8. Important Limitation: Prior Grades and Prediction Timing

UCI explicitly notes that G1 and G2 are strongly correlated with G3 because they are earlier grades from the same course year. Therefore, a model containing G1 and G2 can achieve high explanatory fit partly because it uses information that is temporally close to the final grade. If the practical objective were early identification before G1 or G2 become available, a different model excluding those grades would be required and would likely be more difficult to fit accurately.

Do not overclaim: the high R² should not be presented as evidence that behavioural or demographic variables alone explain student achievement.
9. Conclusion

The fitted multiple linear regression model accounted for a substantial proportion of the observed variation in final grade G3 within this worked dataset. G2 and G1 showed positive adjusted associations with G3, previous failures showed a negative association, and studytime did not show strong evidence of an adjusted association at the 0.05 level. Absences had a small positive fitted coefficient that warrants cautious interpretation. Overall, the model is useful as a teaching example for translating regression output into statistical interpretation; however, its coefficients describe associations within the analysed data and do not establish causal effects.

10. Recommended Reporting Statement

“A multiple linear regression was fitted to examine the association of first-period grade (G1), second-period grade (G2), weekly study time, previous failures and absences with final grade (G3). The overall model was statistically significant, F(5, 643) = 732, p < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶, and accounted for approximately 85.1% of the observed variation in G3 (R² = 0.8506; adjusted R² = 0.8494). G2 (b = 0.8857, p < 2 × 10⁻¹⁶) and G1 (b = 0.1395, p = 0.00013) were positively associated with G3, whereas previous failures were negatively associated with G3 (b = −0.2183, p = 0.0166). Study time was not statistically significant at the 0.05 level (b = 0.0967, p = 0.1182). Absences had a small positive coefficient (b = 0.0234, p = 0.0308), which should be interpreted cautiously. These results describe adjusted associations in the fitted dataset and should not be interpreted as causal effects.”
